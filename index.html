The explainer: what Kelly actually does

One sentence version: Kelly says bet the share of your bankroll that equals your edge divided by the odds. If your edge is bigger, bet more. If the odds pay less per dollar, bet less. If you have no edge at all, bet nothing.

1

Your bankroll is a pot that gets multiplied, bet after bet

If you stake the fraction f of your bankroll and win, your pot is multiplied by 1 + f × b (you keep the stake and add the profit). If you lose, it is multiplied by 1 − f (the stake is gone).

So a $1,000 bankroll with a 10% stake on 2.00 odds becomes $1,100 after a win and $900 after a loss. Long-term results depend on the order and mix of those wins and losses — and on your choice of f.

2

Compounding means you should maximise the average growth rate, not the average profit

Betting too small wastes good opportunities. Betting too big is worse: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain just to get back to where you were. Kelly chooses the stake that makes your bankroll grow fastest compounded over many bets, which is the same thing as maximising the average of log(bankroll).

That is why the growth curve in Chart 1 above has a peak instead of rising forever: past Kelly, extra stake adds risk faster than it adds return, and eventually drags your growth rate down to zero and then negative.

3

The two building blocks: your edge and the odds

edge = (chance you win × profit per $1) − (chance you lose)
Kelly stake % = edge ÷ profit per $1

Edge is your expected profit per $1 staked, expressed as a decimal. An edge of 0.10 means +10% on average for every dollar you risk: stake $1,000 across many identical bets and you expect roughly +$100.

Profit per $1 is the bookmaker's b (also written b, "net odds" or "b to 1"). At decimal odds of 2.50 you win $1.50 per $1 staked, so b = 1.50.

4

The same formula written in each format

f* = (p × b − q) ÷ b    where b = decimal odds − 1, p = your win chance, q = 1 − p
f* = p − q ÷ b    (identical, just rearranged)
f* = (p × decimal − 1) ÷ (decimal − 1)
f* = p − (1 − p) ÷ (decimal − 1) = the form this calculator uses

All four are the same equation. Many sports bettors find the last one easiest: “my win chance minus the break-even chance, divided by the profit per dollar.”

5

Why the answer is a percentage, not a dollar amount

Because your bankroll changes after every bet, and the correct stake changes with it. Kelly is a rule you re-apply, not a one-off decision: after a win your next stake gets bigger, after a loss it gets smaller. That automatic shrinking is what stops a bad run from destroying you.

This calculator multiplies the percentage by the bankroll you entered so you have an actual stake to place, but the percentage is the real answer.

6

Never bet without an edge

If p × b is less than q, the formula returns a negative number. There is no optimal stake — the only winning move is not to play, so Kelly says 0%. This is why the calculator refuses to give you a bet when your win chance is below the break-even rate.

Remember that bookmakers charge a margin, so most bets are negative before you even start. Kelly only recommends a stake when your estimate says the price is too generous.

7

Measuring the reward: expected growth per bet

growth per bet = p × ln(1 + f × b) + q × ln(1 − f)

This is the number under “long-run growth per bet” in the result panel. A growth rate of 0.5% per bet means your bankroll multiplies by roughly 1.005 per bet on average — about 139 bets to double. It is the honest, compounding measure of how good a bet is, and the calculator uses it to tell you how much of Kelly's growth you keep at your chosen aggression level.

8

Half Kelly and friends: buying peace of mind

Betting a fixed share λ of full Kelly (for example λ = 0.5) gives up a little growth and takes away a lot of risk. For small edges the growth you keep is roughly 2λ − λ²: half Kelly keeps about 75% of the growth while halving the swings, and quarter Kelly keeps about 44% with a quarter of the swings.

Risk of a bad drawdown falls even faster. A rough but useful rule: with full Kelly the chance your bankroll is ever cut to a fraction x of its starting value is about x — so roughly a 1-in-2 chance of hitting half at some point, 1-in-4 at half Kelly. The real figures for your exact numbers are simulated live in the result panel.

Kelly stake % = (chance of winning × profit per $1 − chance of losing) ÷ profit per $1  =  (edge) ÷ (profit per $1)

Background: the formula comes from John L. Kelly Jr., a researcher at Bell Labs, in his 1956 paper “A New Interpretation of Information Rate”. Ed Thorp later popularised it for blackjack and markets. It is standard, published mathematics — nothing here is proprietary or paywalled.

How to work out each number (the very basics)

A. Decimal odds, explained from scratch

Decimal odds (also called European or “digital” odds) answer one question: how much do I get back for every $1 I stake, stake included?

profit = stake × (decimal odds − 1)    total returned = stake × decimal odds
  • 1.50 → stake $100, win $50, get back $150.
  • 2.00 → stake $100, win $100, get back $200 (evens).
  • 4.00 → stake $100, win $300, get back $400 (3/1).

Decimal odds are always 1.00 or more. They can never be below 1, because you always at least get your own stake back — a stake of $100 at 1.01 returns $101 for a $1 profit.

B. How to get the true probability (p) — by counting

When the event is a physical object you can literally count the ways it can happen. This is the gold standard, because the answer is exact and not an opinion:

probability = (ways you win) ÷ (total equally likely outcomes)
SituationThe countProbabilityFair decimal odds
Coin lands heads1 way out of 2 sides1/2 = 50.0%2.00
One specific face on a fair die1 way out of 6 faces1/6 = 16.7%6.00
Rolling a 7 with two dice6 combinations out of 366/36 = 16.7%6.00
Rolling a 4 or higher with two dice33 combinations out of 3633/36 = 91.7%1.09
Drawing an ace from a full deck4 aces out of 52 cards4/52 = 7.7%13.00
A named card (e.g. the 7 of hearts)1 card out of 521/52 = 1.9%52.00
European roulette, one number1 pocket out of 371/37 = 2.70%37.00
American roulette, one number1 pocket out of 381/38 = 2.63%38.00
A coin that lands heads 60% of the timemeasured over many flips60%1.67

A casino game pays out on the fair odds minus a slice, which is why the number of pockets matters so much: European roulette pays a single number as if there were 36 pockets (35 to 1, decimal 36.00) when there are really 37, and American roulette pays the same while having 38. That is the entire house edge, and it is why the calculator tells you not to bet.

When you cannot count: sports, politics, weather

For real-world events there are no equally likely outcomes to count, so p is a judgement. Sensible ways to get a defensible number:

  1. Start with the market's fair chance. Enter both sides' odds in the step-3 helper, remove the bookmaker's margin, and use that as your baseline.
  2. Only move it if you have a reason the market has missed: an injury the price has not absorbed, better data, a specialist model, a rule you understand better.
  3. Be humble about the size of your adjustment. Moving a price by more than 2–5 percentage points is claiming a big edge over professionals. If your model disagrees wildly, suspect your model first.
  4. Write the reason down and count your results. If you are wrong more often than you thought, the mistake is in p, not in Kelly.

C. Sanity checks before you place the bet

1

Is my chance bigger than the break-even rate?

If p is below 1 ÷ decimal odds, there is no bet. The calculator shows “no bet” automatically in that case.

2

Does the stake feel absurd?

If Kelly asks for 25% of your bankroll, ask yourself whether a 25%-plus claim over a professional market is really justified. Very large fractions are usually a sign that p is too optimistic, not that you have found a goldmine.

3

Can you survive a losing streak?

At a 10% stake, five losses in a row is a 41% drawdown — and five losses in a row happens to everyone eventually. If that thought makes you panic, lower your fraction of Kelly or set a cap.

4

Can you actually get the bet on at that size and price?

A great number on paper is worthless if the stake is limited or the price moves. If the odds shorten before you get on, come back and recalculate — do not just carry on at the old stake.

Definitions: every number in the calculator

b — profit per $1 staked
b = decimal odds − 1

The reward for a win. At decimal 2.50 you win $1.50 per $1 staked, so b = 1.50. At 6.00, b = 5.00 ("5 to 1").

How to get it: subtract 1 from the decimal odds. If decimal is 4.00 then b = 3.00.

Examples: 1.91 → 0.91  |  2.00 → 1.00  |  11.00 → 10.00
  • If b is small, even a good edge asks for a big stake — the maths is telling you the payout is thin.
p — your chance of winning
a number between 0 and 1 (or 0–100%)

Your honest estimate of the true probability that the bet wins. It is the input that decides everything, and the one most often got wrong.

How to get it: count outcomes when you can (1/6 for a die face, 4/52 for an ace); otherwise start from the market's margin-free price and nudge it only with a real reason.

Examples: coin 50%  |  die face 16.7%  |  ace from a deck 7.7%  |  your sports estimate 45%
q — your chance of losing
q = 1 − p (ignoring pushes)

The other side of the same coin. If you win 45% of the time you lose 55% of the time.

How to get it: 100% minus your win chance. If some outcomes push, remove that share first — a push is neither a win nor a loss.

Examples: p = 45% → q = 55%  |  p = 90% → q = 10%
Decimal odds
total returned ÷ stake

How much comes back per $1 staked if you win, stake included. 2.50 means a $100 stake returns $250.

How to get it: fractional → 1 + top ÷ bottom; American positive → 1 + number ÷ 100; American negative → 1 + 100 ÷ number; percentage → 100 ÷ %.

Examples: 3/1 → 4.00  |  +150 → 2.50  |  −200 → 1.50  |  25% → 4.00
Fractional odds
profit ÷ stake, e.g. 3/1 or 4/5

The old UK format, showing profit only. 3/1 wins $3 for every $1; 4/5 wins 80c for every $1, and you always get the stake back on top.

How to get it: from decimal, subtract 1 and write it over 1: 2.50 → 1.5/1 → 3/2.

Examples: 1/2 → decimal 1.50  |  6/5 → 2.20  |  5/1 → 6.00
American (moneyline) odds
+ underdog, − favorite

Two different questions in one format. A plus number is the profit on a $100 stake: +150 wins $150. A minus number is the stake needed to win $100: −200 means risking $200 to win $100.

How to get it: decimal ≥ 2 → (decimal − 1) × 100 with a plus sign; decimal < 2 → −100 ÷ (decimal − 1).

Examples: +100 = even money = 2.00  |  −110 = 1.91  |  +250 = 3.50
Break-even win rate
1 ÷ decimal odds

The win rate that leaves you exactly level over the long run. At 2.50 you must win 40% of the time to break even; at 1.50 you must win 66.7%.

How to get it: 100 ÷ decimal odds. Then compare with your own p — that gap is your edge.

Examples: 1.50 → 66.7%  |  2.50 → 40.0%  |  11.00 → 9.1%
Edge (also “value”, expected value)
edge = p × b − q

Your expected profit per $1 staked. +0.10 means for every $1,000 you put through identical bets you expect about +$100 on average.

How to get it: multiply your win chance by b, then subtract your loss chance. Positive = bet exists. Negative = walk away.

Examples: p 55% at 2.00 → 0.55×1 − 0.45 = +0.10  |  p 45% at 2.50 → 0.45×1.5 − 0.55 = +0.125
  • The bookmaker's break-even edge is 0: that is exactly the price with no margin.
Kelly fraction (f*)
f* = edge ÷ b

The share of your bankroll to stake for fastest long-run growth. 10% on a $1,000 bankroll means a $100 stake.

How to get it: divide your edge by b. If the result is negative, the correct bet is 0 — never a negative stake.

Examples: edge 0.10 with b 1.00 → 10%  |  edge 0.125 with b 1.5 → 8.3%  |  edge 0.125 with b 0.5 → 25%
  • Short odds make f* big. That is real, not a bug.
Bankroll
the pot the percentage applies to

The whole amount of money you are prepared to play with. Every stake is a percentage of this, so a growing bankroll means growing stakes — that compounding is the whole point.

How to get it: money you have genuinely set aside for betting. Nothing you need for anything else.

Examples: a 10% Kelly stake on $1,000 is $100; on $5,000 it is $500
Stake
stake = f × bankroll

The actual money you put on this one bet. Lose it and it is gone; win and you get it back plus the profit.

How to get it: Kelly % times your bankroll. The calculator does it, and rounds nothing — use your own rounding.

Examples: 8.33% of $1,000 = $83.33  |  25% of $500 = $125
Expected value (EV)
EV = stake × edge

The average profit or loss of this single bet, in dollars. It is what the bet is “worth” to you on average; it says nothing about this particular outcome.

How to get it: stake × edge. A $100 stake with a 10% edge has an EV of +$10.

Examples: $100 stake, 10% edge → +$10  |  $50 stake, −5% edge → −$2.50
Expected growth rate
p × ln(1 + f b) + q × ln(1 − f)

The compounded growth per bet, in log terms. It is the correct ranking of bet sizes, because it accounts for the fact that a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover.

How to get it: the calculator computes it live. 0.5% per bet means doubling in about 139 bets.

Examples: f* 10% on 2.00 odds → +0.501% per bet  |  20% on the same bet → about 0 (double Kelly destroys the edge)
Multiplier (fraction of Kelly)
λ, e.g. 0.5 for half Kelly

How big a share of the mathematically ideal stake you actually bet. 0.5 keeps roughly 75% of the growth for half the swings — the usual professional compromise.

How to get it: pick one. 1.0 for max growth, 0.5 for a smooth ride, 0.25 if you are still learning. Above 1.0 you grow slower and risk more.

Examples: full Kelly 20% → half Kelly 10%  |  quarter Kelly 5%
Drawdown / losing streak
bankroll × (1 − f)ⁿ after n losses

How far your bankroll falls if several bets lose in a row. This — not the expected value — is what most bettors underestimate.

How to get it: multiply your bankroll by (1 − f) once per loss. At f = 10%, three losses take $1,000 to $729.

Examples: f 10% → 5 losses = −41%  |  f 25% → 5 losses = −76%  |  f 50% → 2 losses = −75%
Vig / margin / overround
total implied % of the market − 100%

The bookmaker's built-in profit. If both sides of a two-way market carry 52.4% implied chance, the total is 104.7% and the margin is 4.7%.

How to get it: add up 1 ÷ decimal odds for every possible outcome, then subtract 100%. The fair price is what is left after removing that margin proportionally.

Examples: −110 / −110 (1.91 each) → 104.7%, margin 4.7%  |  a perfectly fair market → 100%, margin 0%
Push / void
outcome where nothing changes hands

A tie or a voided bet returns your stake. It is neither a win nor a loss, so it must be taken out of p and q — otherwise you overstate your edge.

How to get it: estimate how often the event ties. If 4% of games push, split the remaining 96% between winning and losing.

Examples: NFL spread pushes ≈ 2–5% of games  |  a suspended match = void
Full Kelly / Half Kelly
100% or 50% of f*

Shorthand for how much of the ideal stake you use. Full Kelly grows fastest but has the roughest path. Half Kelly is the most common real-world choice.

How to get it: multiply f* by 0.5 for half, 0.25 for quarter. The calculator's aggression selector does this for you.

Examples: f* 8% → half Kelly 4%  |  f* 25% → quarter Kelly 6.25%

Worked examples, start to finish

Every example below follows the same four lines: write down the odds → write down your chance → get b → divide. Press the matching chip at the top of the calculator to load any of them and watch the numbers move.

Coin flip · the simplest case

A coin that lands heads 55% of the time, paying even money (2.00)

This is the textbook Kelly problem. You believe heads comes up 55% of the time and someone pays you $1 for every $1 staked.

odds → b = 2.00 − 1 = 1.00 edge = 0.55 × 1.00 − 0.45 = 0.10 (a 10% edge) f* = 0.10 ÷ 1.00 = 0.10 → 10% $1,000 bankroll → stake $100 (half Kelly: $50)
Answer: bet 10% of your bankroll. Expect +$0.501 of compounded growth per bet — roughly 139 bets to double your money. Bet 20% instead (double Kelly) and your long-run growth falls to about zero: you are taking twice the risk for none of the reward.

Lesson: a 5-point edge over a 50/50 bet is worth 10% of your bankroll, not 50%. Kelly is almost always more conservative than a beginner expects.

Coin flip · thinner payout

The same coin, but paid only 1.80 (4/5)

You still win 60% of the time, but the payout is smaller.

b = 0.80  |  edge = 0.60 × 0.80 − 0.40 = 0.08 f* = 0.08 ÷ 0.80 = 10%  (same stake as the 10% edge at even money!)
Answer: still a 10% stake — but the growth is slower: +0.403% per bet versus +0.501%. Same stake, less reward, because the odds are thinner.

Lesson: the stake depends on edge ÷ b, and the quality of the bet depends on both. Two bets can deserve the same stake while being very different opportunities.

Dice · no edge

Rolling a 6 on a fair die, paid 6.00

“Roll a six and I'll pay you 5 to 1” — that is decimal odds of 6.00. Is it a good bet?

p = 1/6 = 16.67%  |  b = 6.00 − 1 = 5.00 edge = 0.1667 × 5.00 − 0.8333 = 0.8333 − 0.8333 = 0.00 f* = 0 ÷ 5 = 0%
Answer: no bet. The price is fair to within a rounding error, so your expected profit is $0 and Kelly stakes nothing. There is no reward to chase and only variance to collect.

Lesson: fair odds are not an opportunity. You need the payout to be better than fair to have any edge.

Dice · a real (if tiny) edge

Rolling a 6, but paid 7.00 instead of 6.00

A generous friend pays you 6 to 1 on a single face of a fair die.

b = 7.00 − 1 = 6.00 edge = (1/6) × 6.00 − (5/6) = 1.00 − 0.8333 = 0.1667 f* = 0.1667 ÷ 6.00 = 0.0278 → 2.78% $1,000 bankroll → stake $27.78
Answer: a 16.7% edge, but only a 2.78% stake — $27.78 on $1,000. Growth is +0.222% per roll (about 313 rolls to double).

Lesson: big odds (long shots) mean small stakes. The bigger the odds, the more often you lose along the way, and Kelly protects you from that.

Cards · counting outcomes

Drawing an ace from a full deck at decimal 15.00

4 aces in 52 cards = 7.69%. Someone offers 15.00 for it.

p = 4/52 = 7.692%  |  b = 15.00 − 1 = 14.00 edge = 0.07692 × 14.00 − 0.92308 = 1.07692 − 0.92308 = 0.1538 f* = 0.1538 ÷ 14.00 = 0.011 → 1.10% $1,000 bankroll → stake $10.99
Answer: only 1.1% of your bankroll — about $11. Fair odds would be 52 ÷ 4 = 13.00, so 15.00 is genuinely generous and still only justifies a tiny stake.

Lesson: long-odds bets always want small stakes, and the fair price is just the reciprocal of the probability: 1 ÷ 0.07692 = 13.00.

Sports · underdog

A +150 underdog (decimal 2.50) you make 45% to win

The market implies 40% (100 ÷ 2.50). You have researched the match and think 45% is fair.

b = 2.50 − 1 = 1.50 edge = 0.45 × 1.50 − 0.55 = 0.675 − 0.55 = 0.125 (+12.5%) f* = 0.125 ÷ 1.50 = 0.0833 → 8.33% $1,000 bankroll → stake $83.33 (half Kelly: $41.67)
Answer: stake 8.33% of your bankroll, worth +0.515% of compounded growth per bet (about 135 bets to double). Same numbers in the ≤ −200 row below, but notice how different the stake is.

Lesson: five percentage points of disagreement with the market is a 12.5% edge at these odds — that is a big claim. Be honest with yourself about how confident you really are.

Sports · heavy favorite

A −200 favorite (decimal 1.50) you make 75% — and why the stake scares people

The market says 66.7% (100 ÷ 1.50). You think it is really 75%.

b = 1.50 − 1 = 0.50 edge = 0.75 × 0.50 − 0.25 = 0.375 − 0.25 = 0.125 f* = 0.125 ÷ 0.50 = 0.25 → 25% of your bankroll $1,000 bankroll → stake $250
Answer: Kelly says 25% — a quarter of everything, on one game. That is not a mistake: short odds pay so little that the ideal stake is large. Half Kelly would be $125, and many bettors would cap it at 5–10%.

Lesson — the fragility of thin odds: the same bet with slightly different beliefs gives wildly different answers. If the true chance is 75% → 25%. If it is 70% → 10%. If it is 65% → no bet at all. Small errors in p move your stake enormously when b is small, so favour half Kelly or a cap here.

Sports · reading past the vig

Both sides at −110: what is the real chance?

A typical spread market: both sides at decimal 1.91.

implied each side = 100 ÷ 1.91 = 52.4%  →  total = 104.7% bookmaker margin = 4.7% fair chance = 52.4 ÷ 104.7 = 50.0%  →  fair decimal odds = 100 ÷ 50.0 = 2.00
Answer: a coin flip in disguise. The margin-free price is 2.00, so betting one side at 1.91 has an edge of 0.5 × 0.909 − 0.5 = −4.5% per dollar and Kelly says 0%. At the fair price of 2.00 the bet would break exactly even — no profit, no loss.

Lesson: every price contains the bookmaker's margin. Use the “remove the margin” helper in step 3, then decide whether you truly know something the market does not.

Sports · pushes

A spread bet where ties happen: −110 with a 4% push

You think you win 54% of the time, lose 42%, and 4% of games land exactly on the number and push (stake returned).

b = (100 ÷ 110) = 0.909 edge = 0.54 × 0.909 − 0.42 = 0.4909 − 0.42 = 0.0709 f* = 0.0709 ÷ 0.909 = 0.078 → 7.80% wrong (counting the push as a loss): 0.54 × 0.909 − 0.46 = 0.0309 → 3.40%
Answer: 7.8% of your bankroll, once the push is correctly removed from the win/loss split. Handle it the wrong way and the calculator would have told you 3.4% — less than half the right answer.

Lesson: a push is money back, not a loss. It must be excluded from p and q, and it makes your effective edge bigger, not smaller.

Horse racing

A horse at 3/1 (decimal 4.00) you make 35% to win

The market says 25%. You believe your form study says 35%.

b = 4.00 − 1 = 3.00 edge = 0.35 × 3.00 − 0.65 = 1.05 − 0.65 = 0.40 (+40%!) f* = 0.40 ÷ 3.00 = 0.133 → 13.33% $1,000 bankroll → stake $133.33 (half Kelly: $66.67)
Answer: 13.33% of your bankroll. A genuine 40% edge is enormous and yet Kelly still only asks for an eighth of your money.

Lesson: the biggest edges appear where markets are least efficient — and also where your estimate is most likely to be wrong. Remember the track takeout (often 15–20%) is baked into every price, so beating a horse market consistently is extremely hard.

Casino · negative edge

Roulette, single number (pays 35 to 1, decimal 36.00)

European wheel: 37 pockets. American wheel: 38 pockets. Both pay as if there were 36.

European: p = 1/37 = 2.703%, b = 35 edge = 0.02703 × 35 − 0.97297 = 0.94595 − 0.97297 = −0.0270 → −2.70% American: edge = (1/38 × 35) − 37/38 = −5.26% f* = negative → 0%
Answer: never bet. Every $100 wagered costs you $2.70 on a European wheel and $5.26 on an American one on average. Kelly's answer to a negative edge is always zero.

Lesson: if the payout is smaller than the true odds, no staking plan can rescue it. Sizing cannot fix a bad price.

Card counting · small edge, small stake

A 1% edge at even money (the classic blackjack situation)

A card counter believes the deck is 51% in their favour on a hand that pays even money.

b = 1.00  |  edge = 0.51 × 1 − 0.49 = 0.02 f* = 0.02 ÷ 1.00 = 2%  (half Kelly = 1%, quarter Kelly = 0.5%)
Answer: 2% of your bankroll. This is why real card counters bet small multiples of their bankroll even when they know they have the advantage — and why the licensed-professional route is to bet a fraction of Kelly when your estimate of the edge is noisy.

Lesson: a real, verifiable 1% edge is worth about 2% of your money at even money. Anyone promising to double your bankroll with a 1% edge is selling something.

Several bets at once

Two good bets on the same day

You have two independent bets, each with f* = 5% for a $1,000 bankroll. Should each be $50, so $100 total at risk?

independent bets → combined risk is less than the sum of the risks, so 5% each ($50 + $50) is the usual practical answer correlated bets (same team, same result) → treat them as ONE bet and split the one Kelly stake between them, not both
Answer: bet 5% on each if they are genuinely independent, and treat correlated bets as a single position. When your bankroll is being spread across many simultaneous positions, the exact multi-bet solution is more conservative than the sum of the individual Kelly stakes.

Lesson: Kelly is a rule for your total exposure, not a licence to add up individual stakes. Same game, same driver, same correlation — one position.

Cheat sheet — the whole page in ten lines

decimal odds = total returned ÷ stake = 1 + (profit ÷ stake)
fractional → decimal: 1 + top ÷ bottom  |  American +150 → 2.50 (1 + 1.5)  |  −200 → 1.50 (1 + 100 ÷ 200)
break-even win rate = 1 ÷ decimal odds  |  fair decimal odds = 1 ÷ true probability
b = decimal odds − 1  |  edge = p × b − q  |  q = 1 − p (pushes removed first)
Kelly % = edge ÷ b  =  p − q ÷ b  =  (p × decimal − 1) ÷ (decimal − 1)
negative edge → stake nothing, ever
stake = Kelly % × bankroll  |  EV of the bet = stake × edge
growth per bet = p × ln(1 + f b) + q × ln(1 − f)  |  double when cumulative growth reaches ln 2 ≈ 0.693
half Kelly keeps about 75% of the growth with about half the swings  |  above full Kelly you grow slower and risk more
recalculate after every bet: the percentage is the answer, the dollars follow your bankroll

⚠️ Sensible-use note

Kelly is a tool for sizing bets you have already decided have an edge. It is not a way to turn a losing bet into a winning one, and it cannot fix a probability you guessed. Sports betting and casino games can be addictive and are illegal or age-restricted in many places — check your local rules, never bet money you need, and if betting stops being fun, stop. The calculator will happily tell you 0%; that is often the most valuable answer it gives.
